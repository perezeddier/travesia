Do You Need to Speak Spanish in Costa Rica?

The short answer is no — and we can say that with some authority, because we're Costa Rican drivers and most of our passengers don't speak a word of it. But the longer answer is more useful.

The honest answer

Costa Rica has been receiving international visitors for decades, and tourism is a large part of the economy. In the places you're most likely to go, English is normal, not exceptional.

Hotel reception, tour operators, park entrances, restaurants in tourist towns, drivers and guides — these people work with English speakers daily. You will not get stuck.

What changes is the texture of the trip. Without Spanish you'll have a smooth holiday. With twenty words, you'll have a warmer one. That's the real trade, and it's worth understanding before deciding how much effort to put in.

Notice the pattern: English follows tourism. Step one street off the tourist road and it fades — which, not coincidentally, is where the best food and the lowest prices are.

The words that actually change things

Not a phrasebook. These are the ones that do real work, in rough order of value:

If you learn only two: pura vida and con mucho gusto. They're the two that make a Costa Rican smile, and neither is hard to say.

Three things worth knowing about how we speak

  1. "Pura vida" is not a slogan we use for tourists. It's a real, constant part of the language — greeting, farewell, thanks, reassurance, and an answer to "how are you". If it sounds like a marketing line on a T-shirt, that's only because the T-shirts came later.
  2. We say "con mucho gusto", not "de nada". If you learned Spanish elsewhere, this will be the first difference you notice. It's a small thing that locals register immediately.
  3. Costa Ricans are patient with learners. Broken Spanish is met with encouragement here, not impatience. The risk of trying is very low.

Practical tips if you speak none at all

  • Download offline Spanish in your translation app before you travel — mobile signal is patchy in the mountains and on rural roads
  • Save your hotel's name and address in Spanish as a screenshot, not just a booking link
  • Book transport in advance rather than negotiating on the street — it removes the one situation where language most often goes wrong
  • Don't skip the sodas. Point at what someone else is eating and say "eso, por favor". It works, and it's where the good food is
  • Addresses here are described, not numbered — "200 metres north of the church" is a real address. Don't panic; drivers understand it
