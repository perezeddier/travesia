Where to See Sloths in Costa Rica

Most sloth guides list every park in the country. This one is written by people who drive these roads every week, and it starts from a more useful question: where can you see one without changing the trip you already planned?

The thing nobody tells you first

Sloths are not rare. They live across most of Costa Rica's lowland and mid-elevation forest, including trees at the edge of towns and above hotel car parks.

The hard part is not finding a place with sloths. It's seeing one. A sloth is a still, grey-brown shape high in a canopy of still, grey-brown shapes. People walk directly underneath them all day long.

That single fact should drive your planning. The difference between "we saw four" and "we saw none" is almost never the location — it's whether someone who knows the trees was looking with you.

The short version: go to a place where sloths are known and someone is paid to find them. Everything below is a variation on that.

The reliable places, by where you already are

If you're in La Fortuna / Arenal — Bogarín Trail

A regenerated private reserve a few minutes from the centre of La Fortuna, on land that used to be pasture. It's known as one of the most dependable sloth spots in the area, with dozens of resident animals, and the trail is flat and wheelchair-accessible — about 2.5 km.

What makes it the easy answer: it's in town. You don't lose a travel day, you don't drive anywhere, and the guides there find the same animals daily. It's also a serious birding site, with more than 400 species recorded — see our guide to birding stops.

If you're on the central Pacific — Manuel Antonio

Manuel Antonio National Park is the best-known wildlife park in the country, and sloths are among the animals people come for, along with monkeys and iguanas. The park is small, busy and well covered by guides working the entrance.

The trade-off is the crowds. Go early, and take a guide — in a park this busy, the guides are the ones who find the animals while everyone else walks past.

If you're on the Caribbean side — Cahuita and Puerto Viejo

The south Caribbean is thick lowland rainforest and has long been sloth country. Cahuita National Park's coastal trail is flat, easy and walked with guides daily, and the Puerto Viejo area is home to well-known wildlife rescue and sanctuary projects.

Worth knowing: a sanctuary is a different experience from a wild sighting. Both are legitimate — just decide which one you actually want before you book.

That last row matters and it's the thing most guides skip. Northern Guanacaste is dry tropical forest. It's beautiful, but it isn't the habitat where you stumble across sloths the way you do in La Fortuna or on the Caribbean. If sloths are high on your list and your whole trip is Tamarindo and Papagayo, plan a day inland rather than hoping.

Five things that actually improve your odds

  1. Hire the guide. This is the whole game. Guides walk the same trail every day and know which tree an animal has been using this week. It is the single highest-value money you'll spend.
  2. Go in the morning. Not because sloths keep a schedule — they don't — but because that's when the guides are working and the animals shift after the night.
  3. Look for a lump, not an animal. You're scanning for a grey-brown ball wedged in a fork, not a recognisable face. Once you've seen one this way, you start seeing more.
  4. Bring binoculars if you have them. Sloths are usually high. Phone photos of them are famously disappointing; guides carry scopes for exactly this reason.
  5. Don't judge by day one. Over a week-long trip touching La Fortuna and either coast, most people see several without trying very hard.

Please don't do this

You will be offered the chance to hold one, or see one up close somewhere that isn't a licensed rescue centre. Don't. Handling is genuinely harmful to the animal — sloths hide stress rather than show it, so an animal that looks calm often isn't — and the trade in wildlife photo props exists only because tourists pay for it.

A wild sloth twenty metres up, found by a guide who knew where to look, is the better story anyway.

Fitting it into your route

The practical version of all this: sloths shouldn't cost you a day. Bogarín is inside La Fortuna. Manuel Antonio is the reason people go to Manuel Antonio. Cahuita sits on the road you're already driving.
